## Decision

**DCO sign-off is the contributor framework. No contributor licence agreement.**

`LICENSE` now names a copyright holder, `CONTRIBUTING.md` states the sign-off requirement,
`.github/workflows/dco-check.yml` enforces it on pull requests, and that is the whole of the
contributor framework. Item 6 of the release plan is closed.

## Context and alternatives

### This records a decision already taken, rather than making a new one

The mechanism was settled when the sign-off work was commissioned, under a constraint stated at the
time: *sign-off only, no legal review workflow, no signature-collection service, and it must not
obstruct or add friction to the maintainer's own commits.* A CLA fails every clause of that — it
requires collecting and storing signed agreements from each contributor, which is a
signature-collection service whether or not it is automated.

`org/RELEASE_PLAN.md` item 6 predates that constraint and still read *"a contributor licence
agreement before any external pull request"*. This record resolves the contradiction in favour of
the later, explicit instruction, and the release plan is updated to match rather than left
disagreeing with it.

### What DCO gives, and what it does not

DCO is an assertion by the contributor, in the commit trailer, that they have the right to submit
the work under the project's licence. It is the model the Linux kernel uses, and it costs a
contributor one `-s` flag.

Combined with Apache-2.0 the practical gap is narrower than it first appears. Apache-2.0 §5 places
contributions under the licence's own terms unless stated otherwise, which carries the §3 patent
grant with them. So the usual CLA justifications — an explicit licence grant and an explicit patent
grant — are substantially covered by the licence this project already uses.

**What is genuinely not covered, stated plainly rather than glossed:** a CLA typically assigns or
licenses rights broadly enough that the project can **relicense** later without tracing every
contributor. Under DCO alone, relicensing surfaceplate away from Apache-2.0 would require the
agreement of everyone who has contributed. Today that is one person, so the cost is nil. It rises
with each external contributor accepted, and it is not retroactively fixable — a CLA adopted later
binds only those who sign it afterwards.

That is the trade this decision makes: current friction is zero, and the option to relicense
becomes progressively more expensive. It is recorded so that a future maintainer meets it as a
decision that was taken rather than as a surprise.

### Alternatives considered and rejected

- **CLA in addition to DCO.** Rejected: it fails the stated constraint, and it asks strangers for
  paperwork before a project with no external contributors has demonstrated it is worth the
  paperwork.
- **Neither.** Rejected: accepting a patch with no assertion of provenance accepts an unbounded
  legal gap along with the code, which `org/RELEASE_PLAN.md` names as the reason item 6 is not
  cuttable.
- **Defer until the first external contribution.** Rejected: the first external pull request is
  precisely the moment a framework must already exist. Deciding then means either delaying the
  contributor or accepting the patch under a policy written to accommodate it.

## Impact

- **Numerical/model output:** none.
- **Contracts/schemas:** none.
- **Security/data:** none. The DCO check reads commit trailers and nothing else.
- **Operations/release:** external pull requests are accepted on sign-off. Relicensing away from
  Apache-2.0 requires contributor agreement, and that cost grows with contributors.

## Evidence

- `LICENSE:189` reads `Copyright 2026 Pipo Ventures Ltd`, matching `legal-name` in
  `ORGANISATION.md`; `tests/check_identifiers.py` verifies the spelling rather than the line merely
  being non-empty, and its count rose when the line was filled in.
- `.github/workflows/dco-check.yml` runs on pull requests and has passed on every pull request
  raised since it was added.
- No external contribution has been received, so this framework has never been exercised against
  a real contributor. It is preparation, not something proven in use, and item 6's status says so.

## Limitations and follow-up

- **Untested in anger.** The DCO check has only ever seen commits by the maintainer, who is also
  the person who wrote the check. That is not evidence it behaves correctly for a stranger.
- **Sign-off is an assertion, not a verification.** Nothing confirms a contributor holds the rights
  they certify. This is true of DCO everywhere and is not a defect in the implementation.
- **The relicensing cost is a live consequence, not a footnote.** If relicensing ever becomes
  desirable, revisit this record before accepting further contributions, not after.
